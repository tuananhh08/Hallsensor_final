### **ABSTRACT** 

Wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) has emerged as a revolutionary, noninvasive alternative to traditional gastrointestinal endoscopy methods. However, actively controlling and tracking the capsule within the complex human digestive tract remains a significant challenge. This graduation project presents the design and implementation of a high-precision, real-time magnetic localization system for an endoscopy capsule utilizing a Hall sensor array. 

The proposed system seamlessly integrates hardware design and software development: 

- Hardware architecture: A dedicated sensor board was developed, featuring an array of 64 high-sensitivity DRV5055 Hall sensors paired with analog multiplexers. The data acquisition (DAQ) board is driven by a highperformance STM32H7 microcontroller and a 16-bit high-speed AD7689 analog-to-digital converter (ADC) to ensure reliable, high-resolution signal collection. 

- Software and optimization: Embedded firmware was optimized for rapid data capturing and synchronization. On the host computer, a Python-based graphical user interface (GUI) was developed to solve the complex, nonlinear inverse problem of the magnetic dipole model, enabling real-time 5- DoF pose estimation and dynamic 3D trajectory visualization. 

To validate the system's performance, rigorous experiments were conducted using an industrial robot arm (ABB IRB 1200) to establish precise ground-truth trajectories. Experimental results demonstrated mean position and orientation errors of 1.87 mm and 0.99° under constant-orientation conditions, and 2.10 mm and 4.52° under varying-orientation conditions, with an average processing time of 85.15 ms per localization point. These outcomes confirm the stability and reliability of the developed localization framework, proving its high potential to serve as a critical feedback mechanism for future closed-loop electromagnetic actuation (EMA) systems in active capsule endoscopy.

### **Overview of gastrointestinal endoscopy** 

Gastrointestinal endoscopy is an advanced and highly effective medical method for the diagnosis and treatment of diseases related to the stomach and digestive system. Environmental factors such as air pollution, water contamination, and sudden changes in lifestyle, along with improper dietary habits and inadequate rest, are among the main reasons why gastric diseases have become increasingly prevalent. These factors not only have negative impacts on overall health but also create favorable conditions for the development of various stomach-related disorders. 

To accurately and effectively examine and diagnose gastric conditions, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is required to directly observe the interior of the stomach and duodenum. This method allows physicians to directly assess the condition of the gastric mucosa and identify pathological abnormalities such as inflammation, ulcers, polyps, or cancer. During the endoscopic procedure, if any signs of abnormality are detected, tissue samples can be collected from the gastric mucosa for biopsy to determine the presence of cancerous cells or other pathological indicators. This significantly improves diagnostic accuracy, enhances treatment success rates, and contributes to better patient prognosis. Furthermore, gastrointestinal endoscopy is not limited to diagnostic purposes; it can also be used to perform minor therapeutic procedures directly within the stomach, such as polyp removal, hemostasis, or the treatment of certain ulcer cases. These minimally invasive interventions help patients avoid complex open surgeries and reduce recovery time. 

In general, gastrointestinal endoscopy is a crucial method for diagnosing and monitoring disorders of the stomach and digestive system. However, the decision to perform an endoscopic examination depends on the individual patient’s health condition and medical advice from physicians. It is essential for patients to thoroughly discuss their symptoms, medical history, and potential risks with their doctors before undergoing endoscopy. This ensures that the procedure is conducted safely and effectively, providing maximum benefit to the patient’s health.

Magnetic maneuverable capsule endoscopy is an advanced technique that utilizes a capsule containing rare-earth magnetic materials, which operates under an externally generated magnetic field to transmit images from inside the human body to an external receiver. ### **Magnetic capsule control system** 

In 2012, Liao et al. first reported a robotic magnetic capsule endoscopy (MCE) system, developed by Ankon Technologies Co., Ltd. The system consists of a capsule endoscope, a magnet-equipped robotic control unit, a data recorder, 

7 

and a computer workstation with software for real-time visualization and control. In January 2013, the system was approved by the China Food and Drug Administration (CFDA) and became the first commercially available magnetic capsule gastroscopy system worldwide. 

The capsule has dimensions of 28 × 12 mm and captures images at a rate of 2 frames per second, which are transmitted to the data recorder via a sensor array placed on the patient’s skin. The magnetic navigation robot adopts a C-arm configuration and can generate a magnetic field of up to 200 mT, which is significantly lower than the magnetic field produced by a standard 1.5 T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system. 

Using precise external magnetic robotic control, the capsule can be maneuvered with five degrees of freedom, including two rotational and three translational motions. A key feature of this system is its millimeter-scale translational resolution (approximately 2 mm) in three-dimensional space and a rotational resolution of about 3 degrees, enabling accurate navigation of the capsule to virtually any region within the three-dimensional gastric cavity. 

Liao et al. reported a preliminary study conducted on healthy volunteers to evaluate the feasibility and safety of the MCE system for human gastric examinations. The results showed that 79.4% of subjects achieved a gastric mucosal visualization rate of at least 75%, while the visualization rates for the gastric cardia, fundus, body, angular incisure, and pylorus were 82.4%, 85.3%, 100.0%, 100.0%, and 100.0%, respectively. Participants reported good tolerance during both the preparation phase and the examination itself, and no adverse effects were observed. The diagnostic performance demonstrated a strong correlation with conventional gastroscopy (correlation coefficient = 0.765, P < 0.001). These results indicate that MCE achieves diagnostic accuracy comparable to standard gastroscopy and represents a promising non-invasive alternative for gastric disease screening. 

At present, magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy systems are being widely applied in medical practice. However, several critical challenges remain to be addressed, including accurate localization of the capsule within the human body, robust control of the capsule along the gastrointestinal tract, and miniaturization of the capsule to satisfy complex requirements such as tissue biopsy or minimally invasive surgical procedures. 

This project focuses on researching and developing a method to determine the capsule’s position as accurately as possible and transmit this information to a host system, thereby enabling physicians or operators to perform precise and reliable control of the capsule endoscope. 

### **1.3 Capsule endoscopy localization system** 

Research on localization systems for capsule endoscopy is not a new topic, and numerous approaches have been proposed in the literature. Existing localization methods include radio-frequency (RF)–based techniques, magnetic 

8 

sensing, magnetic field modeling, gamma-ray–based methods, and computer vision–based approaches. Son et al. employed an external two-dimensional array of Hall sensors to detect the magnetic field generated by a permanent magnet embedded inside a robotic capsule endoscope (RCE)  [7]. Gamma-ray–based solutions have also been investigated. Than et al. [8] proposed a localization system incorporating three Na radioactive markers with diameters smaller than 1 mm embedded within the capsule shell, which could be detected using a gammaray imaging system. More recently, image-based localization strategies have been widely studied, where consecutive visual images or video streams captured by the onboard camera are used to develop localization algorithms. Marya [9] clinically evaluated the performance of such vision-based localization methods. 

However, these approaches still suffer from several limitations. Gammaray–based methods expose patients to ionizing radiation during examination, raising safety concerns. RF-based localization techniques experience signal attenuation and distortion due to absorption and scattering caused by the electromagnetic properties of human tissues [10]. For magnetic sensor–based methods, particularly those using Hall sensors, the main challenge lies in the requirement to deploy sensor arrays around the human body, as well as the potential interference from external magnetic actuation systems [11]. Nevertheless, unlike gamma-ray or RF approaches, magnetic sensing does not introduce radiation hazards and provides direct physical measurements of the magnetic field generated by the capsule. 

Despite these challenges, Hall sensor array–based localization offers several key advantages. Hall sensors provide high sensitivity, low noise, compact size, and direct measurement of magnetic field components. When arranged in a properly designed array configuration and combined with accurate magnetic field modeling and optimization algorithms, Hall sensor arrays can estimate both the position and orientation of a magnetic capsule with high accuracy. Furthermore, this approach is well suited for integration with magnetically actuated capsule systems, as the magnetic field generated by the capsule magnet can be exploited simultaneously for both actuation and localization. 

Based on a comprehensive comparison of existing localization techniques, this project chooses a Hall sensor array–based magnetic localization method. The proposed system employs an external array of Hall sensors to measure the magnetic field generated by the permanent magnet embedded in the capsule. By solving the inverse magnetic field problem, the five-degree-of-freedom (5-DoF) position and orientation of the capsule can be estimated. This choice balances localization accuracy, patient safety, system simplicity, and compatibility with magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy systems.

### **1.4 Research objectives and scope** 

Overall, accurate localization of the endoscopic capsule is a critically important factor in active capsule endoscopy. It serves as the feedback input of the closed-loop control system, enabling physicians to determine the current position of the capsule, record the locations of polyps or ulcers, and subsequently guide the capsule to the next target regions. Therefore, the urgency and practical significance of this research topic are clear. 

In this work, we have studied and reviewed a wide range of related research papers to develop a magnetic-field-based localization system that does not adversely affect or pose risks to the human body, while still ensuring high accuracy and sufficient coverage of the working space.

### **Magnetic field of a permanent magnet** 

A permanent magnet is a material that generates a stable magnetic field without requiring an external power source. This behavior results from the alignment of magnetic domains within the material, which remain oriented after magnetization. Permanent magnets are widely used in compact electromechanical and medical systems due to their reliability and long-term magnetic stability.

_3.1.3.1. Sensor array spatial layout and spacing pptimization_ 

In the development of our position sensing system, the physical configuration of the sensor array was determined through a practical evaluation of magnetic field behavior. Based on the dimensions of our N52 cylindrical magnet which has 20mm length and 10mm diameter, we have selected a sensor pitch of 20mm to align with the magnet’s physical length. This decision serves as the foundation of our array design, ensuring optimal performance across the entire 140mm x 140mm detection area. The choice for this 20mm spacing is based on the following considerations: 

- Correlation between magnet dimensions and sensing range 

The effective sensing range of a Hall-effect sensor is inherently linked to the magnetic flux density distribution of the target magnet. For a 20mm long cylindrical magnet, the spatial variation of the magnetic field is constrained by its physical geometry. It is important to note that the magnet length is the primary and direct factor governing the spatial range of the magnetic field, whereas other variables like material grade or magnet radius primarily serve to increase the magnetic flux intensity rather than the effective sensing range itself. We found that the sensor pitch must be calibrated to match this spatial characteristic to ensure that the magnet’s magnetic profile is effectively captured as it moves across the array.

### - Maintaining continuity measurement 

To achieve a continuous position output, the system must ensure that the magnet’s magnetic influence is always within the linear operating range of at least one sensor at any given point along the stroke. By setting the sensor pitch equal to the magnet length (20mm), we facilitate a seamless transition between adjacent sensors. As the magnet moves, the signal output from one sensor enters its non-linear region, while the adjacent sensor simultaneously begins to detect the magnetic flux within its own linear region. 

Otherwise, choosing a pitch significantly larger than the magnet length would inevitably result in "measurement gaps" or "blind spots," where the magnetic flux density falls below the threshold required for accurate calculation. Conversely, a pitch that is too tight would unnecessarily increase system complexity and computational overhead without providing a significant gain in spatial resolution. 

- Optimizing the linear operating region 

Our primary goal with the 8x8 array configuration was to maximize the utilization of the linear output region of each Hall-effect sensor. By aligning the sensor pitch with the 20mm magnet length, the design ensures that the system operates primarily within the linear portions of the sensors' sensitivity curves. This alignment minimizes the impact of edge effects—where flux density transitions are non-linear—and simplifies the signal decoding algorithm (e.g., selecting the sensor with the highest absolute magnetic flux value). This leads to a much more stable and reliable position reconstruction across the entire 140mm x 140mm sensing area.

### **3.2 Theoretical model for endoscopy capsule localization system** 

The magnetic localization problem of the capsule endoscope can be divided into two closely related components: the forward problem and the inverse problem. The forward problem describes how a given capsule pose generates the magnetic field distribution measured by the Hall sensor array. In contrast, the inverse problem aims to estimate the capsule pose from the measured magnetic field data. 



### **Forward problem** 

The objective of the forward problem is to establish the mathematical relationship between the capsule pose and the magnetic field distribution measured by the Hall sensor array. In the proposed localization system, a permanent magnet is embedded inside the capsule, while a planar Hall sensor array is employed to measure the magnetic flux density generated by the magnet. 

The capsule pose can generally be described by six degrees of freedom (6DoF), including three translational coordinates and three rotational angles. However, due to the rotational symmetry of a cylindrical permanent magnet around its magnetization axis, the magnetic field distribution remains unchanged when the magnet rotates about its own longitudinal axis. Consequently, the roll angle does not produce any observable variation in the magnetic field measurements and therefore cannot be uniquely estimated from the Hall sensor 

34 

data. To avoid parameter redundancy and improve the observability of the localization problem, the roll angle is excluded from the pose representation. 

As a result, the capsule pose is represented using five degrees of freedom (5-DoF): 𝐏= [X, Y, Z]<sup>𝑇</sup> and two orientation angles (α, β) where α and β denote rotations about the Y-axis and Z-axis, respectively. 

To model the magnetic field generated by the embedded magnet, the magnet is approximated as an ideal magnetic dipole. This approximation is widely adopted in magnetic localization systems because it provides a sufficiently accurate representation for a permanent magnet.

### **Inverse problem** 

The inverse problem aims to estimate the capsule pose from the magnetic field measurements acquired by the Hall sensor array. Unlike the forward problem, which directly computes the magnetic field from a known pose, the inverse problem seeks to recover the unknown pose parameters from measured magnetic data. 

Let **B** meas denotes the magnetic field vector obtained from the Hall sensor array. The objective is to determine the capsule position and orientation that best explains the measured magnetic field. Mathematically, this corresponds to finding the pose parameters (X, Y, Z, α, β) that minimize the discrepancy between the measured magnetic field and the magnetic field predicted by the forward model.

Consequently, a nonlinear optimization algorithm is utilized to estimate the capsule position and orientation by minimizing the error between the measured and predicted magnetic fields. In this work, the Levenberg–Marquardt optimization algorithm is chosen due to its robustness, fast convergence, and effectiveness in solving nonlinear optimization problems. It is suitable for accurately estimating the five-degree-of-freedom (5-DoF) pose parameters from magnetic field measurements.

_4.3.1.1. Experimental setup_ 

During the design and implementation of the Hall sensor-based capsule localization system, various sources of error are unavoidable. The main factors contributing to the localization error can be summarized as follows: 

53 

- Hardware-related errors: These errors may arise from the fabrication and assembly of the Hall sensor array, including sensor placement inaccuracies, misalignment of the PCB layout, variations in sensor sensitivity, offset and gain mismatch among sensors, as well as noise introduced by the data acquisition circuitry and analog front-end components. 

- Magnetic interference and environmental disturbances: The magnetic field measured by the Hall sensor array can be affected by external magnetic disturbances from surrounding electronic devices, ferromagnetic objects, or environmental electromagnetic noise. Such disturbances may distort the measured magnetic field distribution and lead to errors in estimating the capsule position and orientation. 

- Modeling and algorithmic errors: The localization algorithm is based on a nonlinear magnetic dipole model and inverse optimization process. Therefore, approximation errors in the magnetic model, numerical errors in the optimization procedure, interpolation errors, and sensitivity to the initial guess may all contribute to the final localization error.

### **Calibration trajectory design** 

To calibrate the Hall sensor array, a dedicated data acquisition trajectory was designed to estimate the sensor parameters in a stable and sufficiently overdetermined manner. In this study, each Hall sensor is characterized by five calibration parameters, including the offset **,** sensitivity (gain) **,** and the threedimensional sensor position (x, y, z). Since these parameters are coupled nonlinearly in the magnetic field model, the calibration dataset must contain sufficiently rich spatial and angular variations so that the optimization problem becomes well-constrained. For this reason, a grid-based trajectory was selected instead of a purely linear or random motion pattern. The grid trajectory allows the magnetic source to cover the sensing area uniformly, ensuring that all sensors experience magnetic field variations over a broad range of positions and orientations. This improves the identifiability of the sensor parameters and reduces the risk of ill-conditioned optimization. 

In the proposed setup, the Hall sensors used in this work are particularly sensitive to the B **z** component of the magnetic field. Therefore, the calibration trajectory was designed to collect a denser set of measurements in conditions where the variation of the z-component is significant. In the planar directions, the magnetic source was moved on a grid with a step size of 20 mm in both x and y. This value was selected to match the spacing between adjacent Hall sensors in the array. As a result, during the scan, each sensor can be brought close to the region where the magnetic field generated by the capsule is strongest and most informative. In other words, the selected grid spacing ensures that every sensor can operate near its effective detection region, thereby improving the robustness of the calibration process. 

55 

In addition to translational motion, rotational excitation was also included in the calibration trajectory. The pitch and yaw angles of the magnetic capsule varied within the range of (-20 **°** ; 20 **°** ) with a step size of 10 **°** while roll angle was kept constant **.** This angular range was selected based on two considerations. First, because the capsule contains a cylindrical permanent magnet, its magnetic field distribution exhibits partial rotational symmetry, meaning that very large angular sweeps may introduce redundant information while increasing the acquisition time. Second, the range (-20 **°** ;20 **°)** is consistent with the practical angular variation of the capsule in the gastrointestinal environment and corresponds to the region where the inverse localization algorithm remains the most stable and reliable. By limiting the angular sweep to this range while still sampling multiple pitch and yaw configurations, the calibration dataset captures the dominant orientation-dependent behavior of the magnetic field without introducing unnecessary redundancy. 

Based on the design above, the calibration trajectory consists of a total of 5120 measurement points. To ensure a rigorous evaluation of the calibration procedure, the dataset is partitioned into a calibration set and a validation set using a 8:2 ratio, resulting in 4096 points used for parameter estimation and 1024 points reserved exclusively for validation. The calibration set is used to estimate the sensor offset, gain, and position parameters, while the validation set — which is never seen by the optimizer during calibration — is used to assess the generalization capability of the calibrated model on unseen data. This split strategy helps verify that the estimated calibration parameters do not overfit to the calibrated measurements but instead capture the true underlying sensor characteristics. The validation error is subsequently reported in section 4.3.3 to confirm that the calibrated model generalizes consistently across the full workspace. 

Overall, the proposed calibration trajectory was designed to satisfy three main objectives: 

- To uniformly cover the sensing workspace. 

- To excite both positional and orientation-dependent variations of the magnetic field. 

- To provide enough measurements for robust optimization of the sensor offsets, gains, and spatial coordinates. This calibration strategy therefore forms an essential basis for improving the accuracy of the Hall sensor array before performing the final capsule localization experiments.

### **Influence of robot positioning accuracy on localization error**
